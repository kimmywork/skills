# Narrative and Language

Read this when the argument or the wording is not yet doing its job. It covers story structure, the three registers, and the editing discipline. Use it as a prompt, not a formula.

## Audience and takeaway

Before structure, write:

- who is in the room or on the page, and what they already believe;
- the one decision or action this should produce;
- the one sentence they should repeat tomorrow.

If the takeaway needs a paragraph, the presentation is not ready.

## Narrative structures

- **Contrast (Duarte).** Move between the world as it is and the world with the idea adopted. The gap is the argument; the close is the new bliss, not a summary.
- **Answer first (Minto).** For decisions, state the conclusion, then the supporting arguments, then the situation-complication-question-answer that frames them. Executives read the answer, not the journey.
- **Assertion–evidence (Alley).** For technical work, make the headline a sentence that states the claim, and make the body visual evidence. Replace topic labels and bullet walls.
- **Three acts.** For talks, use a setup, a turn, and a resolution. Two turning points are enough; more is a plot.

## Three registers

| Register | Shape | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken | short sentences, repetition, signposts, pauses, concrete examples | reading prose aloud |
| Written | topic sentences, given-new, precision, transitions, citations | undifferentiated wall of text |
| Slide | assertion headline, 6–12 words per line, parallel structure, one idea | prose on a slide |

Do not use one register for another. A report projected on a wall is not a deck; a deck handed over as a report is not a document.

## Classical rhetoric as a discipline

- **Inventio** — find the argument and the evidence.
- **Dispositio** — arrange it: opening, body, close, appendix.
- **Elocutio** — style it: clarity first, ornament second.
- **Memoria** — rehearse it until the structure is in your head.
- **Actio** — deliver it: voice, pace, pause, presence.

Use the canons as a checklist for what is missing, not as a history lesson.

## Clarity

- Active voice; concrete subjects; strong verbs.
- One idea per sentence; cut nominalizations.
- Define a term once; use one term per concept.
- Prefer the concrete example to the abstract claim.
- Signpost: first, second, finally; because, therefore, but.

## Persuasion and ethics

Contrast, tricolon, anaphora, metaphor, and a concrete story are legitimate when they serve the truth. They are not legitimate when they manufacture urgency, cherry-pick data, hide a cost, or mislead a chart. If the audience would object to the framing once they saw the whole picture, cut it.

## Editing

Read it aloud. Cut 20%. Remove hedges and adverbs. Check rhythm. Make headings parallel. Apply the so-what test to every slide and paragraph: if the audience asks "so what?", the point is missing.

## Anti-patterns

- A topic headline that names the subject but not the claim.
- A bullet list where a diagram, chart, or demonstration would be clearer.
- Jargon the audience does not share.
- Ornament that makes the sentence harder to understand.
- A close that summarizes instead of asking for the decision.
