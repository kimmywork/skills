# Presentation

A presentation is an argument an audience can follow and act on. It can be live, written, or both; the medium changes the craft, not the thinking.

## Modes

- **Live** — talk, sharing session, demo, panel or talkshow, workshop, pitch.
- **Written** — report, memo, white paper, one-pager, readout.
- **Hybrid** — deck plus leave-behind, talk plus written summary, recorded talk.

## Thinking

1. **Audience and intent.** Who is in the room or on the page, what they believe, what action or decision this should produce, and what success looks like.
2. **One-sentence takeaway.** Write the sentence the audience should repeat tomorrow. Everything that does not serve it is cut or moved to appendix.
3. **Choose the form.** Live for attention and commitment; written for precision and reuse; hybrid when the decision needs both. Do not project a report or hand over a deck as a substitute for thinking.
4. **Build the argument.** Read `references/narrative-and-language.md`. Use contrast between the world as it is and the world with the idea adopted. For decisions, answer first: SCQA and the pyramid principle. For talks, a three-act arc with two turning points. For technical evidence, assertion–evidence.
5. **Plan the evidence.** For each claim, choose the visual or verbal evidence: story, demonstration, photograph, diagram, chart, short table, or quotation. Prefer evidence over bullet lists.
6. **Design the language.** Read `references/narrative-and-language.md`. Spoken and written registers differ: speak in short sentences with repetition and signposts; write with topic sentences, precision, and transitions; slide copy is an assertion, not prose. Use classical rhetoric as a discipline — invent, arrange, style, rehearse, deliver — not as decoration.
7. **Control structure and time.** Read `references/delivery-and-time.md`. For live: opening, body, close, appendix; a time budget; a change of mode every few minutes; a must-cut list; a Q&A buffer. For written: BLUF, a stand-alone summary, numbered findings, and an appendix.
8. **Design the surface.** Read `references/visual-and-accessibility.md`. Use the design system or visual language that fits this topic, setting, audience, and medium; if none exists, use `references/minimal-visual-system.md` as an adaptable example, not a fixed template. One idea per slide or section; a type scale for the back of the room or the reading distance; honest charts; a grid that survives grayscale.
9. **Rehearse or read back.** For live, run it timed, record it, and cut. For written, read the summary alone and the headings alone; if the argument is not there, rewrite.
10. **Make it accessible.** Read `references/visual-and-accessibility.md`. Alt text, reading order, captions or transcript, color never alone, reduced motion, plain language, and a handout that works without the speaker.
11. **Verify and export.** Render every slide or page, check at presentation size and in grayscale, confirm the artifact opens offline, and confirm the source and the artifact agree.

## Non-negotiables

- **One idea per unit.** A slide, a section, or a paragraph argues one thing.
- **Assertion headline, evidence body.** The headline states the takeaway as a sentence; the body is evidence, not a bullet wall.
- **Legible at the distance.** Body text is large enough for the room or the page; contrast is at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text; changing values use tabular numerals.
- **Honest data.** Every chart states axis, baseline, source, and sample; no truncated axes, no chartjunk.
- **Sources where they appear.** Borrowed data, images, and quotations carry attribution.
- **No animation theatre.** Motion explains change or directs attention; it never delays or decorates.
- **Accessible by construction.** Every meaningful visual has alt text; reading order is intentional; meaning never depends on color alone.
- **Consistent with the system.** Consume the design system's tokens and rules when one exists; otherwise start from the minimal visual system example in this skill, adapt it to the occasion, and mark designed values as designed.

## Output

In the user's language and location: a source outline or Markdown source as the source of truth, the rendered artifact (a self-contained HTML deck for live, a print-ready self-contained HTML document for written, or both for hybrid), and speaker notes, timing, or a reading path. Add a one-page executive brief when the audience needs a stand-alone leave-behind. Keep the source and the artifact from drifting.

## Boundary

This skill designs and writes the presentation; it does not deliver the talk, research the claims, or build the product. Research is a separate research task; reviewing a deck or report is a separate review task; building or governing the visual system is a separate design task.
