# Delivery and Time

Read this when the content is chosen and the question is whether it fits the room, the clock, and the audience's attention.

## Live time budget

- **10% open, 70% body, 20% close and Q&A.** A ten-minute talk is roughly one minute of opening, seven of argument, two of close and questions.
- **One slide per one to two minutes.** More slides usually means more reading, not more content.
- **Three points maximum.** If you have five, you have three and two appendices.
- **Change the mode every five to seven minutes:** story, data, demonstration, interaction, question. Attention drops when the mode stays flat.
- **Use checkpoint slides** ("where we are, where we are going") only when the talk is long enough to need them.
- **Keep a must-cut list.** When time runs short, cut from the list, not from the argument.
- **Store the budget per slide** (`data-budget`) and sum it after edits; the must-cut list lives in the notes.
- **Put a visible timer in the room.** Rehearse to a hard stop, not to "about right".

## Q&A

Listen, repeat the question in one sentence, answer it, check whether it landed, then bridge back to the argument. Prepare the ten most likely questions and one honest answer for each. When you do not know, say so, say how you would find out, and move on. Do not improvise a fact.

## Delivery

Eye contact, pace, pause, volume, and gesture carry more than slide polish. Do not read the slides; the slides are evidence, you are the argument. Rehearse the first thirty seconds and the last thirty seconds more than the middle. Arrive early, test the room, and have a fallback if the technology fails.

## Written time and structure

- **BLUF.** The first paragraph states the conclusion and the recommendation.
- **A stand-alone summary.** A reader who stops after the executive summary has the decision.
- **Reading time.** Estimate 200–250 words per minute; a long report needs signposts, summary boxes, and a short front section.
- **Section headings state conclusions**, not topics.
- **Put depth in the appendix.** Do not make the main path carry every caveat.

## Hybrid

Use one source outline. The deck carries attention and commitment; the report carries precision and reuse. Keep the conclusion, numbers, terms, and recommendations identical. Do not let the deck and the report become two different arguments.

## Anti-patterns

- Overrunning and cutting the close.
- Using Q&A as overflow time.
- A deck with no must-cut list.
- A report with no reading path.
- Rehearsing the slides instead of the argument.
- Treating a recorded talk as a substitute for a written summary.
